How I actually use AI in my design work

Not a hype page. A real, specific breakdown of where AI tools sit in my process — and where they don't.

01

The stack

The tools I reach for most, and what each one is actually for.

Research

Claude

Synthesizing interview notes, spotting patterns across research, first-draft user stories.

Prototyping

Figma AI

Turning rough wireframes into higher-fidelity explorations faster, without skipping the thinking.

Copy

Claude / ChatGPT

Drafting UI copy and microcopy variants to react to — a starting point, never shipped untouched.

Dev handoff

v0 / Claude Code

Quick functional prototypes so engineers can feel an interaction, not just read a spec.

02

Where it fits in my process

AI shows up at specific points — not as a layer over the whole workflow.

01

Synthesizing research

After a round of interviews I paste anonymised transcripts in and ask for candidate themes with the supporting quotes attached. The quotes are the point: a theme I can't trace back to something someone actually said gets dropped. I still do my own pass first, then compare — where the model and I disagree is usually where the interesting ambiguity is.

02

Exploring more directions, faster

Early on, the constraint is rarely quality — it's how many genuinely different directions I can afford to sketch before I commit. I'll ask for eight approaches to a flow, throw out six immediately, and usually find that one of the remaining two is something I wouldn't have reached on my own. The bad options are cheap and they still narrow the space.

03

Stress-testing a decision

Before a design review I ask for the strongest argument against the thing I'm about to present, and for the edge cases I've probably skipped — empty states, slow networks, the user who has 400 of something. It's much less painful to hear a weak spot from a model on Tuesday than from a staff engineer on Wednesday.

04

Closing the gap to engineering

For anything with real interaction behaviour — drag ordering, optimistic updates, a multi-step form that can fail halfway — I build a working prototype instead of annotating a static frame. Engineers can open it, break it, and tell me what I got wrong about the states, which is a much faster conversation than a spec document.

03

A real prompt I use

One example, so this page isn't just abstract claims. Replace with a prompt from your actual workflow.

prompt.txt — placeholder
You: Here are 12 interview quotes about our onboarding flow. Group them into themes, attach the supporting quote to each, and flag anything that contradicts our assumption that users want fewer steps rather than more guidance. If a theme rests on only one quote, say so.

Why this works: the default output of a summarisation prompt is a tidy narrative, which is exactly the thing that makes bad research feel finished. Asking for disconfirming evidence and for weakly-supported themes to be labelled turns it from a summary into something I can actually argue with.

04

What I don't hand off

Being specific about limits is more credible than pretending there aren't any.

Taste and final calls

AI widens the option set; it has no stake in the outcome. Deciding which of eight directions is actually right for this product and these users is a judgement call, and I'd rather own a wrong one than inherit an unowned one.

Talking to real users

Synthesis can be assisted. The interview itself can't. Most of what I learn in a session is in the hesitation before the answer, and none of that survives into a transcript.

Anything shipped without review

Nothing generated reaches a user without me reading every word of it. This is less about quality than accountability: if it ships under my name, I have to be able to defend each decision in it.

The strategic "why"

Choosing which problem is worth a quarter of the team's time depends on context that mostly isn't written down — politics, history, what we tried in 2023. That's the part of the work I'd least want automated.
